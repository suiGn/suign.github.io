THE SECOND INFRASTRUCTURE

Profit ≠ progress. Price is a signal, not a measure of value.

Hypothesis

A significant share of waste and unmet human need comes from a failure of coordination — of architecture — and not only from scarcity. This is a hypothesis, not an axiom: it is stated so that it can be tested, and it can be wrong.

Definitions

S        = set of usable, available resources
D        = set of unmet human needs
M ⊆ S×D  = feasible connections (s, d):
           compatible in location, quantity, time, safety, transport

Π_i      = private profit of actor i
W        = collective welfare

Π_i      = Π_eff + Π_gap
Π_eff    = return on value created (less input per output, better matching)
Π_gap    = return on a difference held: information, access, bargaining
           power, jurisdiction, or risk moved onto others

R(N)     = N(N−1)/2     possible pairwise relations among N participants
R(100)   = 4,950
R(1,000) = 499,500      10× participants → ~100× relations

Real societies are not fully connected. R(N) is an upper bound, used only to show that relations grow faster than participants.

Statements

S1   max Π_i  ⇏  max W      Π_gap can grow while W does not
S2   price(x) → 0   ⇏   utility(x) → 0
S3   expansion  ≠  coordination
  • S1. Profit can reward efficiency, or it can be a rent on a difference: knowing first, being the only one with access, sitting between two sides that cannot see each other, or moving risk onto someone else. The number alone does not say which. The claim is that a significant share of profit today is Π_gap.
  • S2. An unsold apple has price 0 and the same nutrients.
  • S3. The first infrastructure (roads, grids, factories, networks, the Internet) is good at producing and connecting. That is not the same capability as matching what exists to what is needed.

Observations

water        ~2.2 billion people lacked safely managed drinking water (2022)
             → more than 1 in 4 humans                          [2]

wealth       top 10%    ~76% of global wealth
             bottom 50% ~2%                                     [3]
  • Phone vs water. The mobile phone reached most of the planet in about twenty years; safe water has not. The two are not equivalent: a phone is made once, sold everywhere, billed monthly, deployed tower by tower; water needs pipes and treatment in every town, high upfront cost, long-term maintenance and public governance, often for people who cannot pay much. The observation is narrower: where returns are low the market rarely steps in, and where governance is weak, often nobody does. The economy spreads what pays better than what is needed most.
  • Doomsday, 1960. Von Foerster, Mora and Amiot extrapolated historical population growth to a singularity on Friday, 13 November, A.D. 2026 — now weeks away [1]. Real population growth departed from that curve decades ago. What remains is the question: what happens when a system's complexity outgrows the structures built to manage it?
  • Orwell, 1949. In Nineteen Eighty-Four, surplus is destroyed on purpose; ours is destroyed because it is often individually rational — no planner, so no one to remove [4].

Proposed Principle

No usable resource should become waste while a compatible human need remains unmet, when a safe and feasible path exists to connect them. 
∀ s ∈ S, d ∈ D :  (s, d) ∈ M  ⇒  s ↛ waste   while d unmet

Normative, proposed, not proven. It does not ignore economic or physical limits — those are exactly what M encodes. It asks that finding the paths in M be a basic capability, not charity.

Profit should track net value created. Capturing a difference counts only when closing it created value.

Predictions — How It Could Be Wrong

if hypothesis holds:
  better coordination information  → less waste, fewer empty km
                                     without new production

weakens if:
  most waste is explained by safety, legal, or physical limits
  (i.e. M is already small and mostly exhausted)

Example under test: diesel in Mexico.

  • Access gap. A truck brings diesel from Brownsville to San Luis Potosí because that seller closed the sale, while diesel at the same or lower price may be available in Lagos de Moreno that the buyer did not reach. Test: had the buyer seen it, on terms they could use (quantity, timing, spec, contract), would the Brownsville run still happen? If not, the margin was Π_gap.
  • Counter-flow. A buyer in Monterrey gets diesel from the Bajío while another truck carries the same product the other way on the same corridor. Unless grade, contract or timing explains it, this is a coordination failure. If shared visibility does not collapse the flow, the hypothesis fails for this case.
  • Policy gap. Part of a regional price difference can come from pricing rules or subsidies, not coordination. Closing that one is a governance question, not a matching one.

Feasibility is local; the resource is global. Coordination is making M visible across the boundary where it is invisible today, without centralizing ownership.

Patch vs Architecture

defect        → patch fixes it
architecture  → patches accumulate, core function still fails

A system that needs constant external correction — subsidies, donations, emergency aid — to achieve its core function indicates an architecture problem, not a bug. Many of those corrections are essential; the claim is only about what their permanence signals.

Limits

  • Not a claim that markets create no value. Intermediaries consolidate, refrigerate, check quality and finance.
  • Not a claim that one technology solves coordination.
  • Not a claim that every captured difference is bad. Finding a gap is information, and closing one can create value. Separating Π_eff from Π_gap is hard and has to be measured per domain: logistics, finance, platforms.
  • Not a claim that coordination needs a central owner. Putting everyone's data in one system recreates the problem with a new owner; participation should not require surrendering ownership or privacy.
